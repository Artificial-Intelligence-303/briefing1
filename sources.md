# Where to look

Reputable starting points for each area. These are places that publish the underlying numbers rather
than places that write about somebody else's numbers, which is what you want when the assignment asks
for a figure with a unit and a date.

**Navigate from the front page of these sites, or search within them.** Do not paste in a deep link a
chat model handed you: see the warning at the bottom.

Nothing here is a required reading list. It is a starting point so that nobody spends the first hour
of this assignment typing into a search engine.

---

## Area 1: Who is actually using it

Adoption and reach. What share of firms, workers, students, and public agencies report using these
systems, how that differs by industry and place, and how fast it changed.

| Source | What it is good for |
| :--- | :--- |
| **US Census Bureau, Business Trends and Outlook Survey** (census.gov, search "BTOS") | The best adoption number in existence for the United States: about 1.2 million firms, asked every two weeks whether they used AI in the past two weeks and whether they expect to in the next six months. Downloadable, broken out by industry, state, and firm size. Start here. |
| **Stanford HAI, AI Index Report** (hai.stanford.edu/ai-index) | An annual volume of charts with the data behind them released alongside. The adoption and public-opinion chapters are the relevant ones. Every figure cites its own source, so use it as a map to primary data. |
| **Pew Research Center** (pewresearch.org) | Survey data on what the public actually does with these tools and what it thinks of them, with full methodology and sample sizes published. |
| **Eurostat** (ec.europa.eu/eurostat) | The equivalent European figures on enterprise AI use, which lets you say whether a US number is unusual or ordinary. |
| **OECD.AI Policy Observatory** (oecd.ai) | Cross-country comparisons and national statistics collected in one place. |

**Where this area stops:** you are counting who uses these systems. What that does to jobs, wages, and
hiring belongs to area 4.

## Area 2: The environmental bill

Data centre electricity and water, where they are being built and who lives there, emissions, and
grid impact.

| Source | What it is good for |
| :--- | :--- |
| **International Energy Agency** (iea.org), especially *Key Questions on Energy and AI* and the annual *Electricity* report | The reference numbers everybody else quotes for global data centre electricity demand, with the projections stated as projections. |
| **US Energy Information Administration** (eia.gov) | Actual US electricity generation and consumption data, by state and by source, so you can put a data centre's demand next to the grid it sits on. |
| **EPA eGRID** (epa.gov/egrid) | Emissions per unit of electricity, by region. This is how you convert "so many megawatt hours" into "so much carbon", which is the step most news coverage skips. |
| **State public utility commission dockets** (search your state's PUC filings) | Where a utility has to say, in public and under oath, how much power a specific new facility asked for. Local, specific, and almost never reported nationally. |
| **Local newspapers where a facility is being built** | Water agreements, tax abatements, zoning fights, and the people who live next to it. The national story is the aggregate; this is the actual case. |
| **Data centre directories** such as Data Center Map (datacentermap.com) or Cloudscene (cloudscene.com) | Locations and counts, which is what a map needs. Commercial sources, so treat the counts as approximate and say so. |

## Area 3: Following the money

Who is spending, who is raising, who profits, and who pays.

| Source | What it is good for |
| :--- | :--- |
| **SEC EDGAR** (sec.gov/edgar) | Every public company's 10-K and 10-Q, free and searchable. Capital expenditure, segment revenue, and the risk factors section, which is where a company is legally obliged to say what could go wrong. |
| **Company investor relations pages and earnings call transcripts** | Where the capital spending guidance actually gets announced, in the company's own words, with a date. |
| **Bureau of Economic Analysis** (bea.gov) | National investment figures, so you can say what share of total US business investment this spending represents rather than just quoting a large number. |
| **Stanford HAI AI Index, investment chapter** (hai.stanford.edu/ai-index) | Private investment by year, sector, and country, compiled and charted, with its sources named. |
| **Epoch AI** (epoch.ai) | Public datasets on training compute, model costs, and hardware, maintained as data rather than as commentary. |
| **Crunchbase** (crunchbase.com), **PitchBook** (pitchbook.com) | Funding rounds and valuations. Partly paywalled; their free summaries and press releases still carry usable figures. |

## Area 4: People and work

Hiring, displacement, wage effects, what jobs are actually changing, and the people who label the
data.

| Source | What it is good for |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Bureau of Labor Statistics** (bls.gov) | Employment and wages by occupation and industry, job openings and separations (JOLTS), and the occupational projections. The authoritative US numbers, updated monthly. |
| **US Census Bureau, BTOS** (census.gov) | Firms are asked not just whether they use AI but what happened to their headcount. Same survey as area 1, different questions. |
| **World Economic Forum, Future of Jobs Report** (weforum.org) | Employer expectations about which roles grow and shrink, with the survey base stated. Expectations, not outcomes: say so when you cite it. |
| **National Bureau of Economic Research working papers** (nber.org) | Where the serious empirical work on AI and labour appears first. Read the abstract and the tables. |
| **Fairwork** (fair.work), Oxford Internet Institute | Conditions for the data workers and platform workers who make these systems function. The part of this story with almost no coverage. |
| **Partnership on AI** (partnershiponai.org) | Reporting on data enrichment work specifically, including pay and conditions. |

**Where this area stops:** you are following what happens to people and their work. Counting how many
firms use the tools belongs to area 1.

## Area 5: Rules and accountability

Regulation, litigation, liability, procurement rules, and what happens when one of these systems is
wrong about somebody.

| Source | What it is good for |
| :--- | :--- |
| **NCSL Artificial Intelligence Legislation Database** (ncsl.org) | Every state AI bill, enacted and pending, sorted by topic and updated monthly. The count of bills introduced is itself a number you can chart. |
| **Federal Register** (federalregister.gov) and **regulations.gov** | Proposed and final federal rules, plus the public comments on them. You can see who objected and what they said. |
| **CourtListener and RECAP** (courtlistener.com) | Free federal court dockets and filings. Where you read the complaint itself rather than a description of it. |
| **EUR-Lex** (eur-lex.europa.eu) | The official text of the EU AI Act and its implementation timetable, which is the schedule most companies are actually working to. |
| **FTC** (ftc.gov) and **EEOC** (eeoc.gov) press releases and enforcement actions | Concrete cases: who was investigated, for what, and what it cost them. |
| **AI Incident Database** (incidentdatabase.ai) | A public, searchable, dated record of documented AI harms. Countable by year and by sector, which makes it chartable. |
